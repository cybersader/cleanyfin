# Temporary copies and the U.S. Family Movie Act

**Research, not legal advice.** Source review dated November 21, 2026. “Inside” and “outside” below refer to the statute’s **no-fixed-altered-copy condition**, not to blanket clearance under copyright, trademark, or other law.

## The decision in plain language

**Keep playback-only filtering as the baseline. Do not assume deleting edited stream pieces after playback makes them legally transitory.** U.S. law expressly addresses making limited parts of a movie invisible or inaudible during private household viewing, at a household member’s direction, from an authorized copy. The difference that matters here is whether we suppress what the player presents or create a separate, reproducible edited version along the way.

| Design | Where it sits relative to “no fixed copy” |
| --- | --- |
| **Player-side skip or mute** | **Clearly inside on the stated facts.** The player plays the original and suppresses marked picture or sound as it renders. It does not produce a separate, reproducible edited output. That does not validate where the original came from. |
| **Household server, temporary disk segments** | **Uncertain.** Segments written for retry and seek can be read again and stitched together. A segment containing three seconds of movie might live on disk for minutes or an entire session. Deletion at the end does not answer what existed before deletion. |
| **Household server, memory-only pipe** | **Uncertain, but different from a disk cache.** Immediately consumed, overwritten bytes look more like playback buffers. RAM can still hold a fixed copy on some facts. Find out whether the server, proxy, or player can replay or reconstruct edited output and for how long. |
| **Pre-built filtered file kept on disk** | **Clearly outside.** A saved, playable edited movie is the clearest example of what the no-fixed-copy words exclude. Already ruled out by the owner. |

A home NAS is factually different from a company ripping discs and streaming from its own cloud library. But owning the server does not settle fixation, source authorization, or who may receive the stream. Collect the lifetime and replay evidence below before asking copyright counsel to assess a server design. This note does not change Cleanyfin’s [metadata-only project principle](../../../.claude/12-PRINCIPLES.md), which currently excludes Cleanyfin handling A/V even if a household-only implementation might turn out to be lawful.

## What the U.S. statute actually says

The Family Movie Act of 2005 is title II of the [Family Entertainment and Copyright Act of 2005](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/PLAW-109publ9/pdf/PLAW-109publ9.pdf), Pub. L. 109–9, § 202(a), Apr. 27, 2005, 119 Stat. 223. The operative text of [17 U.S.C. § 110(11)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/110), as also reproduced in *VidAngel*, C.D. Cal., ECF 144, PDF stamped p. 12:

> the making imperceptible, by or at the direction of a member of a private household, of limited portions of audio or video content of a motion picture, during a performance in or transmitted to that household for private home viewing, from an authorized copy of the motion picture, or the creation or provision of a computer program or other technology that enables such making imperceptible and that is designed and marketed to be used, at the direction of a member of a private household, for such making imperceptible, if no fixed copy of the altered version of the motion picture is created by such computer program or other technology.

The final paragraphs of § 110 matter too. The definition of making imperceptible excludes the “addition of audio or video content” performed or displayed over existing material or in its place. Omitting or silencing content is the candidate here; adding a replacement clip, dubbed line, or audible beep raises a different question. The separate savings paragraph, reproduced in ECF 144, stamped p. 12:

> Nothing in paragraph (11) shall be construed to imply further rights under section 106 of this title, or to have any effect on defenses or limitations on rights granted under any other section of this title or under any other paragraph of this section.

**Trademark and notice.** [15 U.S.C. § 1114(3)(A)–(D)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/15/1114) is the companion Lanham Act provision, not an additional sentence in § 110(11). (A) limits Lanham Act liability on account of conduct that meets § 110(11). Under (B), the manufacturer, licensee, or licensor of enabling technology must ensure the technology gives “a clear and conspicuous notice at the beginning of each performance” indicating that it is altered from the performance intended by the director or copyright holder. Failure to comply removes the (A)/(B) limitation for the covered technology party. (C) applies the notice requirement to technology manufactured after the 180-day period beginning April 27, 2005. Under (D), failure to qualify creates no inference of trademark infringement. Ask counsel who qualifies as a manufacturer, licensee, or licensor for open-source distribution and how to render the notice on a stock client.

## Fixed does not mean permanent; it also does not mean everything in RAM

[17 U.S.C. § 101](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/101) asks whether the embodiment is “sufficiently permanent or stable to permit it to be perceived, reproduced, or otherwise communicated” and requires that this be possible “for a period of more than transitory duration.” The copies definition allows perception or reproduction “either directly or with the aid of a machine or device” (also quoted in ECF 144, stamped p. 10). The fixed definition also addresses a transmitted work of sounds or images being fixed simultaneously with transmission. These are fixation questions, not exceptions based on where bytes are stored or what their filenames say. Neither the length of a clip nor the fact that an artifact is labeled temporary establishes how long it can be reproduced.

## Filtering disputes and what changed afterward

**Clean Flicks / Huntsman.** [*Clean Flicks of Colorado, LLC v. Soderbergh*, 433 F. Supp. 2d 1236 (D. Colo. 2006)](https://openjurist.org/433/fsupp2d/1236/clean-flicks-of-colorado-llc-v-soderbergh-2533268) arose out of the *Huntsman* litigation. The commercial edit companies delivered edited copies rather than just controlling playback. The later district court *VidAngel* order, stamped p. 14, cites *Clean Flicks*, 433 F. Supp. 2d at 1241, for rejecting transformative fair use where the company removed material and added nothing. The [Copyright Office case summary](https://www.copyright.gov/fair-use/summaries/cleanflicks-soderbergh-dcolo2006.pdf) and the linked opinion are follow-up sources. **UNVERIFIED directly:** the full 2006 opinion was blocked by HTTP 403, and the separate August 17, 2005 *Huntsman* order regarding ClearPlay was not retrieved. Do not turn the edited-copy ruling into a judgment about all player filtering.

**ClearPlay’s contrasting design.** [H.R. Rep. No. 109-33, pt. 1](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CRPT-109hrpt33/html/CRPT-109hrpt33-pt1.htm) is the legislative-history lead for downloadable instructions telling the DVD player what to skip or mute; the filter is data, not a replacement movie. The 2016 *VidAngel* court, stamped p. 20, separately noted record evidence of ClearPlay filtering Google Play while users accessed licensed streams and said the injunction would not prevent such a service. That public-interest observation is not a general authorization. [ClearPlay’s own product page](https://try.clearplay.com/what-is-clearplay/) describes skip/mute and a Chrome app for movies rented or bought through streaming services; it does not verify an exact current provider roster or the implementation’s byte-level behavior.

**VidAngel’s old disc model.** The [2016 C.D. Cal. injunction order](https://cases.justia.com/federal/appellate-courts/ca9/16-56843/24/0.pdf) is reproduced in the appellate excerpts of record, ER001–ER022, district ECF 144. The court found the studios likely to succeed, not that a jury had already decided the merits. On the record before it (stamped pp. 3–4, 6–12):

- VidAngel bought physical discs, removed access controls to copy the titles (CSS on DVDs; the record identifies AACS and/or BD+ on Blu-rays), tagged the content, and stored an encrypted, access-controlled cloud library of roughly 1,300 segments per title, with up to ten seconds of content per segment. Its software assembled the kept pieces at viewing time. Customers nominally bought a disc that VidAngel retained unless shipped and could sell it back. The customer selected at least one filter, but received the stream from the stored digital master, not the purchased disc.
- **Family Movie Act:** the court said, “The digital content that VidAngel streams to its customers is not from an authorized copy.” (Stamped p. 12.) User direction, in-home viewing, and an asserted lack of a saved personalized cut did not cure the unauthorized source. The court treated creating the stored master as a distinct reproduction question, not a holding that every filtered buffer or temporary segment is a fixed altered version.
- **DMCA:** the claim was under [17 U.S.C. § 1201(a)(1)(A)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/1201) (the act of circumvention, not a § 1201(a)(2) trafficking claim). The court rejected the argument that authority to view a purchased DVD authorized decrypting it to copy for the service. It also rejected the argument that the FMA supplies an exemption from § 1201 (stamped pp. 6–8). Its account of the rulemaking was current in 2016; it is not a finding about any individual user’s facts or current exceptions.
- **Public performance and fair use:** the court treated the commercial transmissions to customers as public despite in-home reception, and disc ownership did not make customers owners of the streamed digital master (stamped pp. 10–11). At the injunction stage it rejected fair use: commercial nontransformative entertainment, creative source works, full copies and retention of the works’ heart, and potential licensing-market harm (pp. 13–16).

**Ninth Circuit and later judgment.** [*Disney Enterprises, Inc. v. VidAngel, Inc.*, 869 F.3d 848 (9th Cir. 2017)](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/ca9/16-56843/16-56843-2017-08-24.html) affirmed the preliminary injunction (opinion-index source; full opinion returned HTTP 403, so its exact wording, pinpoint citations, and any reasoning beyond the district order above are **UNVERIFIED** here). The company’s [2020 settlement filed with the SEC](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1671941/000165495420010072/vid_ex13.htm), §§ 1, 6.B, 8.L–M, records the Sept. 23, 2019 $62,448,750 monetary judgment, a $9.9 million scheduled payment amount (with a conditional lower payoff option), an obligation to dismiss appeals, and the Sept. 5, 2019 permanent injunction remaining in full effect. The judgment and the settlement are different numbers. The [permanent injunction docket entry](https://docs.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/california/cacdce/2%3A2016cv04109/650117/520) and the later liability ruling itself were not separately retrieved.

**Proposed law and current products.** [H.R. 6816 (115th Congress), Family Movie Act Clarification Act of 2018](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BILLS-115hr6816ih/html/BILLS-115hr6816ih.htm), introduced Sept. 13, 2018 and referred to Judiciary, proposed a new § 110(12) for private-viewing filter services, including content obtained “directly or indirectly from an authorized copy or digital transmission of the motion picture.” It would have retained the no-fixed-altered-copy condition and supplied limited rules on contracts, technical blocking, and circumvention. It was a proposal, not permission to rely on. Exhaustive later bill history through 2026 was not verified. [VidAngel’s provider help](https://help.vidangel.com/hc/en-us/articles/4402720519437-What-services-link-with-VidAngel) now advertises account linking for supported streaming subscriptions/services, including Netflix and Prime Video, and filtering selected titles through VidAngel; [its other provider guidance](https://help.vidangel.com/hc/en-us/articles/360055396932-Can-I-use-insert-favorite-streaming-service-with-VidAngel) describes exclusions. These are company descriptions, not court approval or evidence of internal storage or decryption behavior.

## Map the implementation to the words

- **Player-side:** A household member sets or affirmatively selects a profile, and a player skips the intervals or gates playback audio from the unchanged source. On those facts the limited portions are made imperceptible *during* the performance, at the household member’s direction. The filter file is not media, and there is no retained edited version. Check all paths for downloads/recording and whether buffering touches edited output rather than merely the original; separately check source authorization, recipient scope, marketing and notice.
- **Server on the fly, disk:** The household’s Jellyfin server keeps the original unchanged and re-encodes around the cut as needed, sends a stream with marked ranges omitted, and might hold an edited playlist and playable chunks in the transcode directory until eviction or teardown. Limited material is omitted, with household direction and private viewing assumed; the statute expressly mentions a performance transmitted to the household. Does producing an edited sequence ahead of rendering count as making imperceptible *during* the performance? Does a playlist and a retained, reconstructible set of segments amount to a fixed altered version even when no one file holds it all? What if only part of the sequence exists at once? Verify every path, not just a clean stop.
- **Server on the fly, memory only:** Same source, omission, and household-directed request, but bounded process/network buffers drain to the client with no disk file. The *during* and authorized-source questions remain. Does it have only continually overwritten data, or can the job hold, seek, resend or replay an edited interval or an entire sequence? Check client caches, HTTP proxies, hardware encoder buffers, tmpfs, swap and crash dumps before describing it as diskless. A RAM-backed filesystem is not necessarily a short-lived FIFO.
- **Pre-built disk file:** May still honor a household’s choice and be made from an authorized original, but creates a fixed altered version and completes the edit before playback. It does not satisfy the no-fixed-copy condition. This is not a conclusion about whether distinct permission or a different defense could apply; Cleanyfin has already excluded the design.

**Separate from fixation:** possession is not the same as authorization of the actual input copy. Ask about a lawful DRM-free download, a licensed stream, and a user-generated disc rip separately. A ripped protected disc raises a separate § 1201 issue; Cleanyfin cannot resolve that for users, does not decrypt and should not supply circumvention tools or guidance. Private household status is not a synonym for LAN/IP address: consider travel access, non-household logins, hosted servers, relays and whether performances are offered to the public. A household member choosing or changing a shared timestamp profile is different from a service imposing an edit. Sharing only timestamps, action/category and release identifiers (no audio/video, stills, or subtitle text) does not itself supply an edited movie or settle what receivers do with the data.

### What ordinary Jellyfin actually retains

Pinned to **v10.11.11**, not a measurement or a promise about a future Cleanyfin path:

- [EncodingOptions constructor, lines 23–26](https://github.com/jellyfin/jellyfin/blob/v10.11.11/MediaBrowser.Model/Configuration/EncodingOptions.cs#L23-L26): `EnableThrottling = false`, `ThrottleDelaySeconds = 180`, `EnableSegmentDeletion = false`, `SegmentKeepSeconds = 720`. **Default is not a rolling 12-minute cap.** With segment deletion off, segment files can remain for playback and until cleanup; with deletion on, 720 seconds is a configured keep parameter, not a measured wall-clock upper bound. Throttling controls production ahead of playback, not deletion.
- [TranscodeManager, v10.11.11](https://github.com/jellyfin/jellyfin/blob/v10.11.11/MediaBrowser.MediaEncoding/Transcoding/TranscodeManager.cs): `StartSegmentCleaner` starts the optional cleaner for eligible HLS jobs (video, file or HTTP input, runtime of at least five minutes) while the job is running. `StartThrottler` is separate and has file/video/runtime and FFmpeg support checks. `KillTranscodingJob` schedules `DeletePartialStreamFiles` after a 1,500 ms delay; `PingTimer` uses 60 s for non-progressive jobs and 10 s for progressive jobs, subject to job/request/progress state. The constructor calls `DeleteEncodedMediaCache` for files under the configured transcode path. None of these is an unconditional lifetime limit on segments: a paused job, a process that exits early, failed deletion, or an unclean shutdown needs separate testing. The exact rolling-cleaner algorithm and crash behavior were not verified. [Jellyfin issue #16608](https://github.com/jellyfin/jellyfin/issues/16608) is a report of fast jobs finishing before the cleanup timer operates, not a tested result for this release or our design.

## Outside the United States

**EU.** [InfoSoc Directive 2001/29/EC, arts. 5(1), 5(5), and recital 19](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX%3A32001L0029) has a conditional reproduction exception: temporary acts must be transient or incidental, integral and essential to a technological process, have no independent economic significance, and serve solely a lawful use or an intermediary’s network transmission between third parties. The [CJEU’s *PRCA v. NLA*, C‑360/13](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/ALL/?uri=CELEX%3A62013CJ0360) concerns on-screen/browser-cache copies, not an edited movie. Moral rights remain a national question: [Germany’s UrhG §§ 14 and 44a](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/englisch_urhg/englisch_urhg.html) separately address distortion/impairment and temporary reproduction; France’s CPI art. L121‑1 is a follow-up integrity provision (**UNVERIFIED** direct text here). A reproduction exception alone does not decide the consequences of filtering under national law.

**UK.** The *PRCA* judgment identifies [Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988 § 28A](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1988/48/section/28A) as the UK’s temporary-copy provision implementing Article 5(1). [CDPA § 80](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1988/48/section/80) is a separate starting point for derogatory treatment; its current consolidated text, trigger and application were not directly verified here. Neither provision should be treated as importing § 110(11); ask UK counsel about the particular playback and distribution design.

## Questions for U.S. copyright counsel before shipping a server path

| Question to decide | Evidence to provide |
| --- | --- |
| Is a disk, RAM or client artifact a fixed altered version? Can separately stored chunks count together? Which forum’s authorities matter, and does the result change if only a cut boundary or only audio is rewritten? | Byte-flow diagram and artifact inventory from source to display; decoded/encoded and original/edited distinction; per-artifact maximum residence (not clip length), max bytes, timestamp of write/read/delete, coexistence across chunks, playlist coverage, and demonstrated replay/reconstruction capability. Include tmpfs, swap, encoder, HTTP cache headers, proxies, and player caches. |
| Does rolling deletion during playback matter compared with deletion at stop? What failure behavior would change the analysis? | Traces for straight play, seek/retry, pause/resume, backgrounding, fast-job completion, concurrent sessions, disconnect, process kill, power loss, restart and failed deletion. Show what remains on disk and in memory, whether it is readable and when it becomes unrecoverable. |
| Does ahead-of-playback boundary re-encoding count as making limited portions imperceptible *during* a performance? How limited is limited, and are any transformations additions rather than omissions? | Timeline showing when encoding begins vs. user playback, amount of work done and retained ahead, edit list range/total coverage, exact skip/mute semantics and any replacement audio/video, overlays or generated material. |
| Which inputs meet the authorized-copy requirement? What should the application say or avoid doing about existing rips, purchased downloads, or licensed streams and separate § 1201 concerns? | Proposed source types, actual byte source for the stream, licensing/provenance assumptions, what the software does and never does regarding access controls, and onboarding/support copy. |
| Is each topology household-directed private home viewing? What changes with travel, sharing credentials, a remote NAS, a third-party relay or managed hosting? Who performs or reproduces what, and what falls outside § 110(11) even if no edited copy is fixed? | Network and trust-boundary diagrams; recipient, account and sharing rules; which entity hosts or touches A/V; how routes, downloads, mirrors and remote sessions behave; who requests playback and chooses/overrides defaults. Show that the shared dataset carries only edit decisions, not excerpts. |
| Who needs the § 1114(3) notice, what should it say, and can every supported client actually present it at the beginning of each performance? | Product positioning, distribution/licensing relationships, stock-client screenshots and start/resume/restart notice behavior; what happens when the client cannot show notice. |
